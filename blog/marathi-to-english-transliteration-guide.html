Marathi to English Transliteration — A Practical Guide

How to convert Devanagari Marathi text into readable English letters, when to use it instead of translation, and which romanization style fits your situation.

8 min read·2026-08-19

Sooner or later, most people who write in Marathi run into a situation where they need their text — a name, an address, a sentence — represented in the Roman alphabet instead of Devanagari script. Maybe it's a form that only accepts English letters, a caption for an audience that doesn't read Devanagari, or simply a keyboard that can't type the script at all. This is where transliteration comes in, and it's worth understanding clearly, because it's a different tool from translation and gets confused with it constantly.

Transliteration vs. Translation — The Core Difference

Translation converts meaning from one language to another. Transliteration converts sound and spelling from one script to another, without touching meaning at all. Take the Marathi word "नमस्कार": translated into English, it means "hello" or "greetings." Transliterated, it becomes "namaskaar" — the same word, same meaning, just written in letters an English reader can sound out.

This distinction matters because the two tools solve completely different problems. If you need an English-speaking friend to understand what a Marathi sentence means, you need translation. If you need to write a Marathi name on a form that only accepts the English alphabet, or you want someone to be able to pronounce a Marathi phrase without reading Devanagari, you need transliteration.

When You Actually Need Marathi-to-English Transliteration

  • Names and addresses on official forms. Passport applications, visa paperwork, and international shipping labels often require names originally written in Devanagari to appear in Roman letters.
  • Captions and subtitles. Adding a readable Roman-letter version of Marathi speech or text for an audience that doesn't read the script.
  • Search and reference. Making a Marathi term findable or typeable in systems built around the English alphabet.
  • Teaching pronunciation. Helping someone learning Marathi sound out unfamiliar words before they're comfortable reading Devanagari script fluently.

There's No Single "Correct" Romanization

One thing that surprises people the first time they try this: there isn't one universally agreed way to romanize Devanagari. A few different conventions exist side by side:

  • Academic/IAST-style transliteration uses special diacritical marks (ā, ī, ś, ṇ) for precise, unambiguous phonetic representation — common in linguistics and scholarly writing, but requires special characters most people can't type easily.
  • Informal/casual romanization — the style most people actually use on WhatsApp and social media — sticks to plain English letters, sometimes doubling vowels to indicate length ("aa," "ee," "oo" instead of diacritics). This is far more practical for everyday typing.
  • Government/official transliteration standards vary by institution — passport offices, banks, and different government departments in India sometimes apply their own specific romanization rules, which may not match either of the above.

Because of this, treat any automated transliteration tool — including ours — as producing a reasonable, readable draft rather than one single "correct" answer. For casual use (captions, informal writing, learning pronunciation), this is rarely an issue. For anything legally formal, check the specific requirement first.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between transliteration and translation?

Transliteration converts how a word sounds from one script into another, preserving pronunciation but not meaning. Translation converts meaning from one language into another. "नमस्कार" transliterates to "namaskaar" but translates to "hello" or "greetings."

Which romanization style should I use for official documents?

Check the specific form or authority's requirements first — passport offices, banks, and government forms in India often have their own required romanization convention, which may differ from the informal style most online tools produce. Treat a free online tool as a starting draft, not a final authoritative source for legal documents.
