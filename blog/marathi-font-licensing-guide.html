Marathi Font Licensing — Can You Use It Commercially?

A plain-English guide to what you're actually allowed to do with a free Marathi font — logos, products, client work — and how to check before you publish.

7 min read·2026-08-19

"Is this font actually free to use?" is one of those questions that seems simple until you need a confident answer for a paying client, a product you're selling, or a business logo. The good news for Marathi typography specifically: almost every font you'll find through Google Fonts — including everything in our own font generator — is released under a genuinely permissive open license. But "almost every" isn't "every," and understanding the actual license terms takes five minutes and saves you from a much more stressful conversation later.

The License That Covers Most Marathi Google Fonts: SIL OFL

The vast majority of Devanagari fonts on Google Fonts — Modak, Baloo 2, Noto Sans Devanagari, Poppins, Hind, Mukta, and most of the others you'll recognize — are released under the SIL Open Font License (OFL), a license specifically designed for fonts by the linguistics organization SIL International. In plain terms, the OFL allows you to:

  • Use the font in any project, commercial or personal — logos, products, client work, apps, paid ebooks, merchandise, without paying a license fee or asking permission.
  • Embed the font in documents, apps, and websites — including embedding it directly in a PDF, a mobile app, or a website's CSS.
  • Modify the font — for your own use, as long as any redistributed modified version carries a different name from the original (so people don't confuse your modified version with the original font).

What the OFL does not allow: selling the font file itself as a standalone product, or redistributing a renamed copy claiming it as entirely your own original work without carrying the license forward. For the overwhelming majority of people — designing a poster, building a website, writing a book, branding a business — none of these restrictions are relevant; you're using the font in something, not reselling the font file itself.

Do You Need to Credit the Designer?

Legally, no — the OFL does not require attribution for using the font in a design or product. That said, crediting the type designer (a small "Typeface: [Name]" note in a portfolio piece, or a mention in project credits) is considered good etiquette in the design community and costs you nothing, especially for larger commercial projects where the font plays a visible, central role.

Where Licensing Gets Genuinely Murkier: Legacy Fonts

This is the one area worth real caution. Older, pre-Unicode Devanagari font systems — Kruti Dev, Shree Lipi, DevLys, and similar legacy fonts widely used in Indian government and DTP contexts for decades — often have licensing that's less clearly documented, distributed informally, or tied to a specific commercial vendor rather than an open-source foundation. If a project needs airtight commercial licensing, it's safer to migrate legacy content to a properly open-licensed Unicode font rather than assume an old, informally distributed legacy font file is clear to use commercially. A Quick Checklist Before You Publish

  • Check the font's specific license page on Google Fonts (each font lists its license directly) rather than assuming — the vast majority are OFL, but it takes thirty seconds to confirm.
  • For legacy/pre-Unicode fonts, verify the source before commercial use, or migrate to an open-licensed Unicode font instead.
  • Keep it simple: if you're using the font, not reselling the font file itself, you're almost certainly fine under an OFL-licensed font — this covers the overwhelming majority of real-world use cases.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use Google Fonts for a client's logo or paid commercial project?

In almost all cases, yes — the vast majority of Google Fonts, including the Devanagari fonts in our font generator, are released under the SIL Open Font License, which explicitly permits commercial use, including selling products that use the font, without paying royalties or asking permission. Always check the specific font's license page to confirm, since Google Fonts occasionally hosts fonts under slightly different open licenses.

Do I need to credit the font designer when I use a free Devanagari font?

Under the SIL Open Font License, attribution is not legally required for using the font in a design, document, or product. It is, however, considered good practice and appreciated by type designers, especially for larger commercial projects.

Is a legacy font like Kruti Dev free to use commercially?

Licensing for older legacy fonts like Kruti Dev and Shree Lipi varies by version and distributor and is often less clearly documented than modern open-source fonts. If commercial use matters, it's safer to migrate to a properly open-licensed Unicode Devanagari font, and use our converter to bring old Kruti Dev content into Unicode first.
